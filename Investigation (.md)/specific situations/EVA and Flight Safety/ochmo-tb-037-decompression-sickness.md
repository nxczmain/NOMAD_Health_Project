### Executive  Summary

Decompression Sickness (DCS) also known as “the bends”, is a mission-critical medical risk caused by the formation of inert bubbles during rapid reductions in ambient pressure. In spaceﬂight, the most signiﬁcant pressure drops occur during transitions from cabin pressure to the lower pressures of extravehicular activity (EVA) suits. Several factors can increase an individual's susceptibility to DCS, including the presence of a patent foramen ovale (PFO), body fat %, hydration, and exercise type. DCS is eﬀectively mitigated through a prebreathe protocol and if not properly managed, it can lead to musculoskeletal pain, neurological impairment, cardiopulmonary compromise, cognitive impairment, risk to mission outcomes/success, and death.

### Decompression Sickness Mechanism

Decompression sickness can be understood by the basic gas law principle described by Henry’s Law, which states that the amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is directional proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid.

C= concentration of dissolved gas k= Henry’s Law Constant 𝐶𝐶 = 𝑘𝑘𝑘𝑘 P= partial pressure of gas

At cabin pressure, historically 14.7 psia, nitrogen equilibrates between the lungs and blood, dissolving in plasma and diffusing into the tissues. At the higher pressure, each breath contains more compressed air molecules resulting in a buildup of nitrogen, a physiologically inert gas.

When ambient pressure is rapidly reduced, (e.g., transitioning from cabin pressure to an EVA suit, historically 4.3 psia) the partial pressure of nitrogen falls rapidly, and this sudden equilibrium disruption forces an increase rate of nitrogen elimination. If the off-gassing exceeds the rate of elimination via exhalation, nitrogen starts to form bubbles in blood and tissues which is where the manifestation of DCS symptoms occur. Bubbles may appear in extravascular (tissue or interstitial) and intravascular compartments. There are two main types of intravascular bubbles:

## Pathophysiology

Diagnosing Decompression Sickness. Divers Alert Network, 2020

Although the pathophysiology of DCS is not entirely understood, it includes right to left shunting of VGE via several mechanisms, one of which is a patent foramen ovale (PFO). A PFO is a shunt between the right and left atrium of the heart and is a remnant feature of the natal heart. All humans are born with a PFO, but approximately 75% of the PFOs fuse following childbirth (Steiner, 1998). With the remaining 25% of the population whose PFO does not fuse, approximately 6% are considered to be large (>2 mm) and the diameter can also increase in age (Hagen, 1984). A PFO raises concern because theoretically a VGE may pass from the right to left atrium, bypassing normal lung filtration, which can allow bubbles in the arterial system leading to a neurological event.

Currently, NASA does not screen out individuals with PFOs. For more information: Assessment of Patent Foramen Ovale (PFO) as Related to Decompression Sickness (DCS) in the Spaceflight Environment and During Ground Testing,

Understanding PFO. Divers Alert Network, 2021

### Predisposing Risk

DCS has many predisposing factors such as hydration, body fat %, and exercise type.

Hydration Hydration plays a significant role in DCS risk. Dehydration reduces plasma volume and can increase blood viscosity, leading to impaired inert gas transport and decreasing bubble removal from the body. Conversely, proper hydration promotes effective gas washout and reduces DCS.

Conkin et al., 2013

### Body Fat Percentage

Higher body fat contents can increase DCS risk because nitrogen is significantly more soluble in fat than in water. Higher nitrogen contents stored in fat leads to excessive nitrogen bubble formation thus increasing DCS risk.

Wang et al., 2020

Exercise Pre-exposure exercise promotes removal of inert gasses by increasing circulation and tissue perfusion. Contrarily, vigorous or resistive exercises after decompression can exacerbate bubble growth by promoting tissue microtrauma and increasing perfusion gradients.

Webb JT, et al., 2003

### DCS Detection

DCS is typically diagnosed based on symptom presentation because the presence of VGE or AGE does not necessarily indicate DCS. Many individuals have detectable VGE’s without symptoms, while others may have symptoms without detectable bubbles. Generally, low bubble loads are associated with lower DCS risk and high bubbles are associated with higher risk, but DCS risk associated with bubble count accuracy is location dependent where the subclavian sites are more accurate compared to precordial sites. The bubble load (quantity and persistence of bubbles) may be used to indicate decompression stress, therefore potential DCS risk can be identified using the Eftedal-Brubakk (EB) scale.

Conkin et al., 2013 Time of VGE and DCS onset in 78 exposures in which both VGE and DCS were present (solid curve) and in 150 exposures in which only VGE was present (dashed curve). The curves, all of which are skewed to the right, are the best-imposed normal distributions on histograms.

The EB scale grades VGE presence through doppler or ultrasound imaging. The NASA-modified scale ranges from 0-7 each identifying different bubble loads and its correlated DCS risk. Higher grades on the EB scale indicate an increased DCS risk.

### EVA Workload

High intensity workload especially involving the lower body during an EVA due to increased perfusion in large muscle groups, such as ambulation, can promote bubble formation in tissues due to increased circulation. Proper workload management is critical in DCS risk management.

### Suit Pressure

In-suit pressure determines the differential pressure between the habitat which directly impacts DCS risk and prebreathe duration. Higher suit pressures reduce DCS risk, but limits dexterity and endurance while lower suit pressures significantly improve mobility but has an increased risk of nitrogen bubble formation.

Garbino et al., 2023

### Preabreathe Duration

To mitigate DCS risk in spaceflight, a prebreathe (PB) protocol of variable amounts of time is used prior to a decompression event. PB works by eliminating nitrogen inhalation in ambient air by replacing it with 100% oxygen, therefore reducing alveolar nitrogen partial pressure. With alveolar partial pressure nearing zero, nitrogen from tissues diffuse across the gradient between alveoli and tissue and is then mobilized into the circulation and subsequently expired, progressively decreasing total body nitrogen. Over time, this reduces supersaturation at the time of decompression by limiting bubble production, therefore reducing the overall DCS risk. The effectiveness of PB is dependent on maintaining a zero-nitrogen alveolar gradient which means any mask leak, seal breach, or glove/suit configuration issue that allows the crewmember to inhale ambient cabin air is classified as a break in prebreathe (BiPB). Even brief BiPB rapidly reintroduces nitrogen into arterial blood, disrupting washout and elevating bubble growth risk. Operationally, a BiPB imposes steep compensatory PB time or can force a complete protocol restart, putting the entire EVA timeline at risk.

### Denitrogenation Prebreathe

To define the true dose through diffusion-based physics and mass-balance, NASA uses the Tissue Bubble Dynamics Probability (TBDM) model, a biophysical model of bubble growth. This model applies mathematical and statistical analyses to estimate the likelihood and severity of bubble formation in tissues. When inputting relevant durations, rates, pressures and gas compositions, the model outputs a bubble growth index (BGI). The BGI is the time varying ratio of bubble radius to an initial 3-µm radius of the bubble. This is calculated throughout the exposure, and the peak value is typically used as the primary measure of decompression stress.

BGI provides significant prediction of DCS and VGE data (p < 0.1). While TBDM models offer valuable predictive insights, they exhibit substantial variability and are validated through human testing.

### Past NASA Decompression Protocols for Prevention of DCS

NASA has never experienced a Type II event in spaceflight

In-Suit 4-Hour Prebreathe

Astronaut breathes 100% O2 in the spacesuit at 14.7 psia for 4 hours.

### Campout Protocol

Significantly reduces the required in-suit prebreathe duration by having EVA crewmembers “camp out” in the ISS airlock at 10.2 psia, 26.5% O2 during the night prior to their EVA. For various operational reasons, the time at 10.2 psia is limited to 8 hours and 40 minutes.

### Exercise Protocol

Intense, short exercise regimen at 14.7 psia while breathing 100% O2 combined with in-suit prebreathe at 10.2 psia.

In-Suit Light Exercise (ISLE) Protocol

A longer period of mild exercise in the EMU. The ISLE protocol shares many steps with the exercise prebreathe protocol but differs in that 40 minutes are spent breathing 100% O2 by mask followed by a 20-minute depressurization to 10.2 psia.

### Future Options

NASA is currently exploring alternative parameters and conditions through the Exploration Atmosphere (EA) study to shorten the amount of time spent on PB. These test simulate the pressure transition during cabin to suited pressure changes, and the results focus on nitrogen washout efficiency, DCS risk mitigation, and operational feasibility. The results from these test will inform the Artemis mission, suit design constraints, and risk posture to further ensure prebreathe protocols are both effective and efficient.

- Future Option: Working PB

The Working (or "Step-Down") Prebreathe Protocol is a staged decompression strategy designed to mitigate the risk of DCS during EVAs. Unlike resting PB, the step-down approach pairs an elevated initial spacesuit pressure with active surface task execution, incrementally stepping down suit pressure throughout the duration of the EVA. By beginning the EVA at a higher pressure, initial decompression stress and bubble growth risk are kept low when tissue nitrogen is at its peak. - [V2 6009] Decompression Sickness Treatment Capability The system shall provide DCS treatment

capability.

- [V2 6017] Atmospheric Control The system shall allow for local and remote control of atmospheric

pressure, humidity, temperature, ventilation, and ppO2.

- [V2 6022] Atmospheric Monitoring and Alerting Parameters The system shall display real-time values for

pressure, humidity, temperature, ppO2, and ppCO2 data to the crew locally and remotely.

- [V2 7043] Medical Capability A medical system shall be provided to the crew to meet the medical

requirements of NASA-STD-3001, Volume 1.

- [V2 9053] Protective Equipment Protective equipment shall be provided to protect the crew from

expected hazards.

- [V2 11100] Pressure Suits for Protection from Cabin Depressurization The system shall provide the

capability for crewmembers to wear pressure suits for sufficient duration during launch, entry, descent (to/from Earth, or other celestial body) and any operation deemed high risk for loss of crew life due to loss of cabin pressurization (such as in mission dockings, operations during periods of high incidence of Micrometeoroids and Orbital Debris (MMOD) or complex vehicle maneuvers).

- [V2 11032] LEA Suited Decompression Sickness Prevention Capability LEA spacesuits shall be capable of

operating at sufficient pressure to protect against Type II decompression sickness in the event of a cabin depressurization.

- [V2 11036] Suited Atmospheric Monitoring and Alerting Suits shall monitor suit pressure, ppO2, and

ppCO2 and alert the crewmember when they are outside safe limits.
