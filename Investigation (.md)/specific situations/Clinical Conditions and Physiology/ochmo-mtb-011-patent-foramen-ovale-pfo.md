## Physiology

A patent foramen ovale (PFO) is one of the most common types of congenital heart defects. During pregnancy, the fetal lungs are non- functional and receive oxygenated blood from the placenta. As the heart develops, a hole/shunt (PFO) located between the right and left atrium of the heart is present allowing blood to pass, directly bypassing the lungs.

Post-natal increases in left atrial pressure usually force the inter-septal valve against the septum and permanently fuse within the first 2 years of life (Saary & Gray, 2001). In 25% of the population the fuse is not established, and the PFO remains open usually undetected and harmless.

The concern with PFOs is that right to left shunt between atria can allow venous emboli to pass from the right atrium (venous) to the left atrium

### Spaceflight Risks for PFO

The risk of PFO in spaceflight presents during extravehicular activities (EVAs) when astronauts perform mission tasks outside the spaceflight vehicle wearing a pressurized suit at a lower pressure than the cabin pressure. PFO can increase the risk of decompression sickness (DCS) as the right to left shunting of venous gas emboli (VGE), increases risk of potentially harmful embolism migration. DCS mitigation protocols are based on strategies to reduce bubbles causing systemic nitrogen load. These are implemented through the combination of habitat environmental parameters, EVA suit pressure, and breathing gas procedures (prebreathe protocols) to achieve safe and effective mission operations.

Agitated saline bubble study The bubble study is a noninvasive test that allows observation of blood flow through the heart. It consists of a regular echocardiogram (an ultrasound that shows the heart structure) accompanied with an injection of agitated saline solution into the right atrium, which in an unaffected heart the bubbles will travel to the right ventricle and are diffused in the lungs. If a PFO is present, the bubbles will travel from the right atrium to the right ventricle showing evidence of a shunt or hole in the heart. The practitioner will apply pressure to the abdomen or encourage the patient to Valsalva to increase pressure that will allow the bubbles to be more visible across the PFO. If a PFO is detected through a bubble test, the patient may undergo more invasive diagnostics to further confirm PFO and determine size. Other tests including TTE and TEE also use bubbles to identify PFO.

Patent Foramen OTviatllee (PFO) OCHMO-MTB-011 Detection & Classification Transthoracic Echocardiogram (TTE) A noninvasive method using a probe on the skin of the chest, similar to a standard ultrasound echocardiogram. TTE can be used with contrast and/or agitated bubbles. TTE can identify PFO or other cardiac pathologies, however views may be limited due to distance from transducer and image quality may be suboptimal in some patients. The TTE result sensitivity may be limited in patients with high BMI compared to a transcranial doppler (TCD) (Liou, 2015). The risk of TTE procedure is low, and the most common adverse effects is discomfort from the IV/injection and potential skin irritation from the transducer transmission gel. TTE is often used by cardiologists as the initial diagnostic test due to low risk, ease of use, low cost, and diagnostic ability (Van der Giessen, 2020; Fordyce, 2022).

Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) Gold standard for PFO diagnosis in patients with stroke, with a weighted sensitivity of 89% relative to autopsies or inspection during cardiac surgery. TEE places a probe with an ultrasound transducer into the esophagus to assess structure and function of the heart. The transducer is located closer to the atria, allowing higher frequency ultrasound than TTE resulting in higher resolution images. One limitation is that the transducer may interfere with the patient’s ability to perform an adequate Valsalva maneuver which can help identify a PFO.

TEE may require light sedation and is time consuming, more costly, and more invasive compared to either TCD or TTE, including risk of esophageal tear during procedure (0.03-0.09%) and reaction to sedating medication. TEE has been considered the diagnostic modality of choice for many institutions to more definitively rule out or further characterize a PFO after an initial assessment via TTE.

### Treatment Guidelines

PFO Closure (Transcatheter) Closure of a PFO is performed using a transcatheter device fed into the heart via the femoral vein. During this procedure, a transeptal sheath travels through the PFO into the left atrium, an umbrella‐like structure opens left atrium side of the septum, and then the right atrium side, creating a seal over the PFO so blood can no longer traverse the septum (the closure device does not create a seal immediately after the procedure, the endothelium must migrate over the device to accomplish a true seal). The patient is usually prescribed oral anticoagulation for 3 – 6 months post-closure. Complication rates with PFO closure are dependent on the device utilized and skill of the clinician. Complications reported by various trials include atrial fibrillation (3-7%) cardiac tamponade (0.4%), device migration/embolization (0.5%), infective endocarditis (0.2%), and stroke (0.4%). These potential complications are rare, but all should be mentioned in the patient consent process for device closure. Gonnah et al., (2022).

In a randomized open-label trial of 980 patients with PFO and history of cryptogenic ischemic stroke, long-term (>5 years) follow-up found that closure was associated with a 45% lower rate of recurrent ischemic strokes than among patients who received medical therapy alone. Closure reduced the relative risk of recurrent cryptogenic stroke by 70% compared to medical therapy. ## Pathophysiology

### PFO Risks in the Diving Community

The diving community is one population that may be affected by a PFO. Undersea divers experience pressure changes which can cause a buildup of nitrogen bubbles. A PFO can increase risk that these bubbles will travel directly into the arterial bloodstream leading to embolization in organs and tissues. The PFO in divers may let gas bubbles from venous blood pass the arterial side, increasing risk of DCS. Divers Alert Network (DAN). DCS is caused by a decrease in pressure surrounding the body. Divers experience nitrogen gas compression during ascent; the nitrogen bubbles enlarge and travel throughout the body. The enlarged bubbles can migrate to lungs, joints, or other body areas causing blockages, discomfort, neurologic symptoms, and potentially death. The known risk factors for divers are deep or long dives, cold water, heavy exercise at depth, and rapid ascents.

- Source: Osmosis.org

Conservative diving includes reducing dive times, limiting number of dives, use of nitrox with dive planning tools, lengthening safety stops, more shallow dives, and avoid heavy exercise and

### Divers Alert Network

Most standard guidelines have a stratified approach that suggest recreational and commercial divers do not need to be screened for PFO, as serious DCS is rare and the link between PFOs and DCS is not clear. The DAN Guidelines for Patent Foramen Ovale recommends “Routine screening for PFO at the time of dive medical fitness assessment is not indicated. Consideration should be given to testing for PFO when there is a history of more than one episode of decompression sickness (DCS) with cerebral, spinal, vestibulocochlear or cutaneous manifestations.” At the NASA Neutral Buoyance Laboratory (NBL), astronauts are trained to conduct suited/pressurized extravehicular activities (EVA) underwater to simulate a microgravity environment. Support divers also participate in underwater activities to observe and assist astronauts during training. Since 2015, 79,568 dive hours (including astronauts and support divers) have been conducted with a total of 5 events of DCS recorded. Divers are not screened for PFOs at this time.
