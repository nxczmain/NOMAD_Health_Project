Comments and limitations:

1. General comments. This CLiFF characterizes small bowel obstruction (SBO) that could occur

during spaceflight.  Small bowel obstructions are a relatively common cause of an acute abdomen in terrestrial studies.  The risk of SBO increases significantly from adhesions after previous abdominal surgery.  There are a number of other predisposing factors such as hernias and metastatic cancer.

However, astronauts have been well-screened for such conditions and SBO is unlikely to occur de novo during spaceflight.  Large bowel obstruction (LBO) is not considered in this CLiFF as some of its major causes such as diverticulitis and constipation are the subject of separate CLiFFs.  Astronauts have also been screened for other etiologies of LBO such as cancer and inflammatory bowel disease.

Diagnosing SBO from other causes of abdominal pain can be challenging without XR or CT; however, ultrasound, which will be carried onboard, has been shown to have excellent sensitivity and specificity in the hands of a trained operator, nearly as good as CT.  SBO may respond to conservative management with IV fluids, bowel rest, and nasogastric suction, but surgical management may be required.  There have been no reports of SBO or LBO during spaceflight.  The best case is considered to be an SBO which responds to conservative medical treatment such as bowel rest, fluid resuscitation, analgesics and NG tube.  The worst case is considered to be an SBO that is not responsive to conservative management and requires surgical intervention.

2. Incidence. There have been no reports of SBO during spaceflight; therefore, analog data was

used from an analysis of health data from Polaris submariners.  The population of screened, healthy adults in an isolated environment is thought to be a reasonable analog of spaceflight.  Two cases of SBO were reported during a period of over 12,000 person-years.  Since this is often a painful condition, it is unlikely that SBO is under-reported. While this study is from the 1970’s and diagnostic and surgical techniques have improved over the intervening years, there is no terrestrial evidence to indicate that the incidence of SBO has changed since the time of the survey.  The best case/worst case probabilities are calculated from terrestrial studies showing a range of 10-25% of admissions for SBO that required surgery, yielding a BC/WC probability split of 75-90%/10-25%.  The clinical phase durations, RTDC and Loss of crew life probabilities are supported by terrestrial evidence and by the condition definitions.  The probability density curve that was derived appears to be a reasonable representation of the data presented.

3. Clinical phase data.  I am in agreement with the clinical phase durations entered in this CLIFF.

This data was compiled by a team of expert clinicians, and I was involved in the discussions leading to the calculation of the clinical phase durations.

4. Task impairment.  The analysis was performed and reviewed by a separate team of expert

clinicians.  I am aware of the methods that were used but did not directly participate in the discussions.

5. Dependence on mission phase or duration of mission. This medical condition should not be

dependent on mission phase.

6. Limitations

- This CLiFF only covers small bowel obstruction and not large bowel obstruction.  Some

causes of LBO are considered in other CLiFFs such as constipation and diverticulitis; however, this is not a comprehensive list.  In future iterations, combining SBO and LBO into a single CLiFF entitled “Bowel Obstruction” may be considered.

- There have been no reports of SBO during spaceflight; therefore, an analog study was used

to calculate incidence.  Although submariners, like astronauts, are healthy, screened adults, there may be differences in the spaceflight environment that predispose astronauts to SBO.

It is uncertain how SBO would present and respond to treatment in microgravity and partial gravity environments.
